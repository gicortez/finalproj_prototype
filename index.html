The Sky Is Falling

An explorable explanation of NASA's fireball data

By Christopher Nguyen, Athena Lin, Gian Cortez, and Mac Dang (DSC 106)

Atlas Energy Atmosphere Missing data Data and methods Prototype writeup

A fireball is a very bright meteor. NASA's Center for Near Earth Object Studies keeps a list of them that was detected by U.S. government sensors. We used a snapshot of that list with 1,050 events from 1988 to 2025 to look at where fireballs are detected, how much energy they release, and what the data leaves out.

Most fireballs are small

89% of the events in the record release less than 1 kiloton (kt) of estimated energy, but a few are much bigger. The largest event, Chelyabinsk, is 37% of the total energy in the record.

Fireballs happen high in the atmosphere

Takeaway

Fireballs are detected all over the planet, and a few large events make up much of the energy. The record is also incomplete. We can describe what was reported, but we can't use it to estimate the real rate of fireballs or where they are most likely to happen.

Data and methods

  • Source: NASA/JPL CNEOS Fireball Data API (version 1.2). We saved one snapshot on November 11, 2026, with events up to December 31, 2025. It has 1,050 events. NASA's fireball introduction and API documentation.
  • Measurements: Times are in UTC. Latitude, longitude, and altitude are for the moment of peak brightness. Energy is NASA's approximate impact energy in kilotons of TNT. None of the charts estimate ground damage.
  • Our choices: Missing values stay missing. All 1,050 events are in the energy and yearly charts, 864 events with a location are in the maps and hemisphere chart, and 578 events with an altitude are in the scatterplot. The controls in the atlas only change the atlas. The map outlines come from Natural Earth and the charts are made with D3.js.
  • Downloads: cleaned data (CSV), original API response, snapshot notes, proposal draft, static figures (zip), static figures (PDF).
Data dictionary

The CSV has 12 columns: an event ID, the UTC date, the year, estimated impact energy (kt), radiated energy (10^10 J), signed latitude and longitude (degrees), altitude (km), velocity (km/s), and three velocity components (km/s). The ID and year are columns we made. The other columns come straight from the API, except that we used the N/S and E/W columns to make latitude and longitude negative where needed. We did not fill in or remove any values.

Energy shares add up the estimated energy of every event in the snapshot. The histogram uses equal-width bins on a log scale. The scatterplot's energy axis is also logarithmic, and the ranking chart is linear. The hemisphere chart counts latitudes above 0 as northern and below 0 as southern. The globe hides the far side of the Earth, so use the world map to see every located event at once.

NASA notes that the list is not complete and only has the brightest fireballs. Reporting, sensor coverage, which measurements are available, and revisions to old events can all change what we see. We do not estimate a global rate, a land and ocean split, or any link between altitude and energy. The place names for Chelyabinsk, the Bering Sea, and Sulawesi were added by us.

Prototype writeup

What have you done so far?

We picked the NASA/JPL CNEOS fireball dataset and saved one snapshot of it with 1,050 events from 1988 to 2025. Christopher and Mac researched the dataset and cleaned it into a CSV, and Athena and Gian checked the cleaning and made the six static figures for the proposal. We worked together on the D3 page, which has a globe and world map of the 864 events with a location, an energy histogram, the five largest events, and an altitude scatterplot, plus interactions like rotating the globe, a timeline slider, and a threshold slider. Because the repo is on Gian's GitHub account, he committed the files and turned on GitHub Pages, but we all went through the changes together before publishing. All four of us tested and debugged the page and worked on the text and README. The page goes from where fireballs are detected, to how big they are, to what the data leaves out.

What will be the most challenging part of your project to design and why?

The hardest part will be making the page a story and not just a pile of charts. A lot of the data is missing, since 186 events have no location and 472 have no altitude, so we have to show that clearly without it looking like a bug. Energy ranges from about 0.01 kt to 441 kt, so we need a log scale, and we have to explain it so readers get it quickly. Chelyabinsk is so much bigger than everything else that it makes the other events look tiny, so we added a toggle to remove it. The globe was also tricky technically, because it has to stay smooth while rotating with hundreds of dots and still show tooltips on hover. Our next step is linking the charts so a choice in one view updates the others, which will take the most time, and with four people in one repo we also have to be careful about who edits which file.
